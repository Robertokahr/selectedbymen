# Selected by Men — Editorial & Article Standards

**Internal Editorial Guide**
**Version 1.0 — August 2026**

> **Buy fewer things. Own better ones.**

This document defines how articles published by **Selected by Men** should look, sound, and behave.

The goal is not to produce the largest number of articles. The goal is to make every article feel **selected, considered, useful, honest, and unmistakably part of the Selected by Men brand.**

---

# 1. The Editorial Principle

Selected by Men is not a product database.

It is not a catalog of Amazon products.

It is not a content farm designed around keywords.

It is an editorial filter.

The reader should feel that someone has already done the tedious part:

* identifying the relevant options;
* comparing them;
* understanding their differences;
* considering their real-world tradeoffs;
* eliminating weaker choices;
* and explaining why the remaining products deserve attention.

The article should therefore answer one central question:

> **Why did this product earn its place here?**

Not:

> "Why does this product have good specifications?"

And not:

> "Why does Amazon sell this product?"

---

# 2. The Three Things Every Article Must Deliver

Every article should provide three layers of value.

## 2.1 Information

The reader needs accurate, useful information:

* specifications;
* materials;
* features;
* compatibility;
* price positioning;
* warranty;
* maintenance;
* ownership considerations;
* relevant limitations.

## 2.2 Judgment

Information alone is not curation.

The article must interpret the information.

For example:

> A larger battery is not automatically better if the device takes proprietary accessories.

Or:

> A premium material matters less if the product is uncomfortable to use every day.

The reader should understand **what the information means**.

## 2.3 Decision support

The article should help the reader decide what to do.

That means answering questions such as:

* Who should buy this?
* Who should skip it?
* What problem does it solve?
* What are the compromises?
* Is the premium justified?
* What would make another option better?
* What should the reader consider before purchasing?

The article should reduce uncertainty.

---

# 3. The Selected by Men Voice

The tone is:

**confident, restrained, intelligent, practical, editorial.**

It should feel closer to a well-edited men's magazine than to a personal blog or SEO content site.

## The voice should be:

* direct;
* calm;
* opinionated without being theatrical;
* precise;
* occasionally dry or witty;
* comfortable saying "no";
* never desperate to sell.

## The voice should NOT be:

* excessively casual;
* hyperbolic;
* breathless;
* salesy;
* corporate;
* artificially luxurious;
* full of empty adjectives;
* written like an Amazon listing.

---

# 4. Elegant and Personal Are Not Opposites

Selected by Men does not need to sound anonymous.

But personality should come from **editorial judgment**, not autobiography.

### Avoid:

> "Hi, I'm John and I've always been passionate about men's grooming."

### Prefer:

> "A six-blade foil is not automatically a better choice. For someone who shaves twice a week, it may simply be more machine than problem."

The second version has a human point of view without requiring a personal biography.

---

# 5. The "We" Must Mean Something

Using "we" is acceptable and desirable for the brand.

But "we" must never become an empty pronoun.

Avoid repeatedly saying:

> "We think this is great."

Instead, explain **why**.

For example:

> "We favor this model because it combines a replaceable head, a strong warranty, and a simpler ownership cost than several more expensive alternatives."

The reader should always be able to ask:

> "What evidence produced that judgment?"

And find an answer.

---

# 6. Never Pretend to Have Tested Something

This is one of the most important rules.

If Selected by Men has not physically tested a product, **do not imply that it has.**

Avoid statements such as:

* "We used it for months."
* "We tested it."
* "We wore it."
* "We use this every morning."
* "After a year of use..."
* "It survived our testing."

unless that experience actually happened.

Instead, clearly distinguish between:

### Hands-on testing

The product was physically tested.

### Research-based evaluation

The selection is based on:

* manufacturer specifications;
* manuals;
* warranty information;
* verified customer feedback;
* product documentation;
* ownership costs;
* comparative research;
* market information.

This distinction should never be hidden.

A research-based article can be excellent.

**Pretending it is hands-on is what damages trust.**

---

# 7. Transparency Is Part of the Product

Transparency should not feel like a legal disclaimer bolted onto the bottom of an article.

It should be part of the editorial identity.

When relevant, tell the reader:

* what was evaluated;
* what was not physically tested;
* what sources informed the decision;
* when the information was last checked;
* whether prices can change;
* whether availability can change.

A compact methodology block can be used consistently.

### Suggested format

**Our methodology**

We evaluated these products based on materials, performance-related specifications, ownership costs, warranty, verified customer feedback, and value relative to competing options. Products were not physically tested unless explicitly stated.

**Last reviewed:** [date]

---

# 8. Every Article Needs a Clear Editorial Thesis

Before writing, identify the article's actual point.

The article should not simply be:

> "Here are five products."

It should have an editorial argument.

Examples of arguments:

* The most expensive option is not necessarily the best value.
* A simpler product may be better for most people.
* One technology is better suited to a specific type of user.
* A premium price is justified only under certain conditions.
* A popular product has a significant compromise that buyers should know about.

The article should feel like it **reached a conclusion**, not merely collected information.

---

# 9. Recommended Article Architecture

The exact structure can vary, but the following framework should be the default for commercial guides.

## 9.1 Editorial opening

Start with the problem, not the product list.

The opening should establish:

* why the category is confusing;
* what actually matters;
* what Selected by Men is trying to solve.

Avoid opening with generic statements such as:

> "In today's fast-paced world..."

The reader already knows what the product category is.

Get to the point.

---

# 10. The Intro Should Establish the Filter

A strong introduction should communicate the editorial philosophy.

For example:

> "There are dozens of electric shavers that promise a closer shave, better comfort, or smarter technology. Most differences matter less than the marketing suggests. We narrowed the field to the models that make a meaningful case for their price."

This does three things:

1. establishes expertise;
2. explains why the list exists;
3. creates a reason to keep reading.

---

# 11. "How We Picked" Should Be Standard

This is one of the strongest elements of the Premium Electric Shavers article and should become a recurring Selected by Men component.

It should explain **what actually influenced the selection**.

Depending on the category, this can include:

* materials;
* construction;
* performance-related specifications;
* ergonomics;
* durability;
* maintenance;
* replacement parts;
* warranty;
* ownership cost;
* compatibility;
* customer feedback;
* price;
* availability.

Do not use the exact same checklist for every category.

The methodology should adapt to the product.

For a shaver, battery life and replacement heads matter.

For clothing, fabric, construction and care may matter more.

For electronics, compatibility, battery degradation and repairability may matter more.

The framework is consistent.

The criteria are contextual.

---

# 12. Explain What Was NOT Evaluated

This is equally important.

If something could materially influence a purchase decision but was not evaluated, say so.

For example:

> "We did not conduct laboratory noise measurements."

or:

> "These selections are research-based rather than hands-on tested."

This prevents accidental overclaiming.

It also demonstrates editorial discipline.

---

# 13. The Product Selection Should Have Roles

Avoid creating a ranking that feels arbitrary:

1. Product A
2. Product B
3. Product C
4. Product D
5. Product E

Instead, give products **specific jobs**.

Examples:

* Best Overall
* Best Value
* Best Premium
* Best for Sensitive Skin
* Best Compact Option
* Best for Travel
* Best for Beginners
* Best for Long-Term Ownership

The exact labels depend on the category.

The important principle is:

> **Every selected product should earn its position for a reason.**

---

# 14. Every Product Needs a "Why"

A product section should answer:

### Why is it here?

Not simply:

> "It has a 60-minute battery."

Instead:

> "The 60-minute battery matters because it removes one of the small daily inconveniences that makes cheaper models frustrating to own."

Connect the specification to the experience.

---

# 15. Every Product Needs a "Tradeoff"

This is essential.

Never make every recommendation sound perfect.

For each product, identify its principal compromise.

Examples:

* expensive replacement heads;
* heavier design;
* proprietary charger;
* weaker warranty;
* higher upfront cost;
* unnecessary features;
* less convenient maintenance;
* limited compatibility.

A credible recommendation says:

> **This is good, and here is what you give up to get it.**

---

# 16. "Who This Is For"

This is one of the strongest components from the Premium Electric Shavers article.

Use it whenever relevant.

### Who this is for

Explain the user profile that benefits from the product.

### Who should skip it

Explain who would be better served elsewhere.

This is much more useful than generic "Pros and Cons."

It forces the recommendation to become contextual.

---

# 17. The "Who Should Skip It" Rule

Selected by Men should be comfortable telling readers **not to buy something**.

This is a major trust signal.

If every article ends with:

> "This is an excellent choice for everyone."

the editorial filter becomes meaningless.

A strong article should occasionally say:

> "Skip this if..."

That may reduce an individual conversion.

But it increases the credibility of the entire site.

---

# 18. Cost of Ownership Matters

One of the strongest ideas in the shaver article is the distinction between:

> **price of purchase**

and

> **cost of ownership.**

Whenever relevant, evaluate:

* replacement parts;
* consumables;
* cartridges;
* batteries;
* proprietary accessories;
* maintenance;
* subscription requirements;
* repairability;
* expected replacement cycles.

A cheap product that requires expensive consumables may not be cheap.

A more expensive product that lasts longer may have better value.

The reader should understand that distinction.

---

# 19. Do Not Confuse "Premium" With "Better"

Selected by Men should never equate:

> expensive = better.

Premium products should earn the premium.

Whenever a product costs substantially more, answer:

### What does the extra money actually buy?

If the answer is meaningful:

> better materials, better construction, better ergonomics, lower ownership friction...

say so.

If the answer is mostly:

> branding, unnecessary features, cosmetic upgrades...

say that too.

---

# 20. Use Comparative Judgment

Specifications become much more useful when compared.

Instead of:

> "This model weighs 220g."

Prefer:

> "At 220g, it is noticeably lighter than several of the premium alternatives."

The purpose of specifications is not to reproduce a manufacturer's product page.

It is to help the reader understand **differences that matter**.

---

# 21. The Article Should Not Become a Specification Dump

Avoid long lists of numbers without interpretation.

Bad:

> Battery: 60 minutes
> Charging: 1 hour
> Weight: 215g
> Waterproofing: IPX7
> Motor: 10,000 RPM

Better:

> "The IPX7 rating makes it suitable for rinsing under running water, while the 60-minute battery is enough for several weeks of normal use."

Facts should serve decisions.

---

# 22. Use "What We Left Out"

This is another strong element from the existing articles.

Whenever appropriate, explain notable products that were considered but excluded.

Examples:

### What we left out

> We considered X, but its replacement heads cost substantially more and its advantages over the cheaper option were too narrow.

This demonstrates that selection actually occurred.

It shows the reader:

> **There was a filter between the market and this list.**

---

# 23. Avoid Fake Completeness

Do not imply:

> "These are the only good products in the world."

Prefer:

> "These are the products that currently make the strongest case within our criteria."

The market changes.

Products disappear.

Prices move.

New models launch.

The editorial selection should be presented as **current judgment**, not eternal truth.

---

# 24. Make Updates Visible

Articles should show when they were reviewed.

Recommended:

> **Last reviewed: August 28, 2026**

When appropriate, also state what was reviewed:

> Prices, availability and product specifications checked.

Do not use fake historical dates.

If an article is published today, its publication date should reflect today.

Do not backdate content merely to make the site appear older.

---

# 25. Never Create a False History

The site should never imply that it has existed longer than it actually has.

Avoid:

* invented publication dates;
* invented testing periods;
* invented editorial history;
* claims of years of experience that do not exist.

A new publication is allowed to be new.

**Honesty is more valuable than artificial authority.**

---

# 26. Recommended Product Section Template

Each major recommendation can follow a structure like this:

## [Product Name]

**Best for:** [specific user/problem]

### Why we picked it

A concise editorial explanation.

### What stands out

The features that genuinely matter.

### The tradeoff

The main compromise.

### Who should buy it

The appropriate user.

### Who should skip it

The wrong user.

### Ownership

Relevant information about maintenance, replacement parts, warranty, accessories or long-term costs.

### Verdict

A short, decisive conclusion.

### Check price

Affiliate CTA.

Not every section needs every heading visibly displayed, but the underlying questions should be answered.

---

# 27. Calls to Action

Affiliate links should be obvious without becoming aggressive.

Good:

> **Check price on Amazon**

> **See current price**

> **View on Amazon**

Avoid:

> **BUY NOW!!!**

> **GET THIS AMAZING DEAL!!!**

> **DON'T MISS OUT!!!**

The brand is editorial.

The CTA should feel like a natural continuation of the recommendation.

---

# 28. Affiliate Disclosure

The reader should understand that Selected by Men may earn a commission.

The disclosure should be clear and accessible.

Do not hide the commercial relationship behind vague language.

The site should be able to say, plainly:

> Selected by Men may earn a commission from qualifying purchases made through links on this site.

The commercial relationship does not need to be apologetic.

It needs to be transparent.

---

# 29. Amazon Should Not Dictate the Editorial Voice

Amazon is the commercial infrastructure.

It is not the editorial identity.

Do not write articles that feel like Amazon product pages.

Selected by Men should explain:

> **why the product matters**

rather than simply:

> **what Amazon says the product does.**

---

# 30. Do Not Manufacture Independence

There is no need to pretend that Selected by Men is completely independent from Amazon.

Instead, be transparent.

If Amazon is the primary retailer because of:

* product availability;
* customer reviews;
* convenience;
* broad selection;
* purchasing infrastructure;

say so.

Credibility comes from honesty, not pretending the business model does not exist.

---

# 31. Images

Visual identity matters significantly for Selected by Men.

Images should feel:

* restrained;
* editorial;
* masculine without clichés;
* tactile;
* clean;
* premium;
* believable.

Prefer:

* products on real surfaces;
* controlled lighting;
* natural environments;
* close product details;
* restrained lifestyle imagery;
* compositions that support the article.

Avoid excessive:

* hyper-muscular models;
* generic stock photography;
* exaggerated luxury imagery;
* fake product demonstrations;
* obviously artificial campaign scenes.

The visual language should support:

> **Buy fewer things. Own better ones.**

---

# 32. AI-Generated Images

AI imagery may be used where appropriate, but it must not create false evidence.

Never use an AI image to imply:

* a real hands-on test;
* a real person using the product;
* a real physical environment;
* a product configuration that does not exist.

If the product itself is being represented, accuracy takes priority over aesthetics.

A beautiful but misleading image is worse than a simple accurate one.

---

# 33. SEO Should Support the Editorial Experience

SEO is necessary.

But SEO should not dictate how the article feels.

The goal is:

> **Optimize the search result without making the page feel optimized.**

Use the target query naturally in:

* title tag;
* H1 when appropriate;
* introduction;
* relevant H2s;
* URL;
* image alt text where genuinely descriptive;
* body content.

Do not force keywords into every paragraph.

---

# 34. Title Tag vs. H1

The title tag can be more explicitly search-oriented.

Example:

> **Title:** Best Electric Shavers for Men in 2026: 7 Worth Buying

The H1 can have slightly more editorial character:

> **H1:** Electric Shavers Worth Owning

But clarity comes first.

Do not make the H1 so clever that the reader cannot immediately tell what the page is about.

A good rule:

> **The search engine should understand the topic. The reader should understand the promise.**

---

# 35. Avoid Factory Titles

Do not turn the site into a repetitive pattern of:

* 5 Best X in 2026
* 7 Best Y in 2026
* 10 Best Z in 2026

Commercial roundups are useful.

But vary the editorial formats.

Possible formats include:

* Best X
* X vs Y
* Is X worth it?
* X for [specific user]
* What we would buy
* What to skip
* X buying guide
* How much should you spend on X?
* The real cost of owning X
* X that earns its place

The variety should feel editorial, not algorithmic.

---

# 36. Internal Linking

Every article should connect naturally to related content.

Internal links should help the reader move through a topic.

For example:

**Category guide**

→ specific product roundup

→ comparison

→ buying guide

→ ownership article

→ related category.

Do not add links merely because SEO plugins recommend a certain number.

Every internal link should answer:

> **Would the reader genuinely benefit from clicking this?**

---

# 37. The Article Should Have a Logical Flow

A strong commercial article generally moves through:

**Problem**

↓

**What matters**

↓

**Methodology**

↓

**Selection**

↓

**Comparison**

↓

**Tradeoffs**

↓

**Who each product is for**

↓

**Final recommendation**

The reader should feel that the conclusion was earned.

---

# 38. The Conclusion Should Make a Decision Easier

Do not end with:

> "We hope this guide helped you."

Instead, summarize the decision.

For example:

> If you want the strongest balance of performance and ownership cost, choose X. If comfort is your priority, Y makes the better case. If you're not willing to pay for replacement heads, skip both and consider Z.

The conclusion should compress the article into a usable decision.

---

# 39. A Good Article Does Not Need to Be Extremely Long

Length is not a quality metric.

A 1,500-word article that answers the reader's questions is better than a 4,000-word article padded with:

* generic introductions;
* repeated specifications;
* filler;
* unnecessary history;
* keyword variations.

Write until the decision is adequately supported.

Then stop.

---

# 40. Every Article Should Pass the "Why?" Test

Before publication, ask:

### Why this product?

Answered.

### Why this ranking?

Answered.

### Why this price?

Answered.

### Why should someone choose it?

Answered.

### Why should someone NOT choose it?

Answered.

### Why trust this selection?

Answered.

If these questions cannot be answered, the article is not finished.

---

# 41. The Credibility Test

Before publishing, ask:

> **Does anything on this page contradict what Selected by Men claims to be?**

Check for:

* fake dates;
* unsupported first-person claims;
* fake testing;
* exaggerated claims;
* inaccurate specifications;
* generic AI-looking images;
* unexplained rankings;
* inconsistent methodology;
* hidden commercial relationships;
* broken links.

One contradictory signal can undermine several good ones.

---

# 42. The "Would We Say This Offline?" Test

Read every strong opinion and ask:

> **Would an editor actually defend this sentence if challenged?**

If the answer is no, rewrite it.

Avoid language like:

* revolutionary;
* perfect;
* unbeatable;
* game-changing;
* flawless;
* the ultimate;
* must-have;

unless there is a very strong reason for using it.

Selected by Men should sound confident enough not to need hype.

---

# 43. The "Would We Recommend It Without the Commission?" Test

This should be one of the strongest internal checks.

Ask:

> **If this product paid us exactly the same commission as every other product, would we still rank it here?**

If yes, the recommendation has editorial integrity.

If no, reconsider it.

---

# 44. The "Buy Fewer Things" Test

Every article should ultimately reinforce the site's philosophy.

Ask:

> **Does this article help the reader buy better, or does it simply encourage the reader to buy more?**

Selected by Men should be comfortable recommending:

> one excellent product

instead of:

> five mediocre products.

It should also be comfortable telling someone:

> **You probably don't need this.**

That is part of the brand.

---

# 45. Standard Article Checklist

Before publishing any article, confirm:

## Editorial

* [ ] The article has a clear thesis.
* [ ] The selection has a reason.
* [ ] Every recommended product has a role.
* [ ] Tradeoffs are explained.
* [ ] "Who should buy" is clear.
* [ ] "Who should skip" is clear where relevant.
* [ ] The conclusion makes a decision easier.
* [ ] The article sounds like Selected by Men.

## Methodology

* [ ] What was evaluated is explained.
* [ ] What was not tested is clear.
* [ ] No hands-on experience is implied without evidence.
* [ ] Sources are appropriate.
* [ ] The selection criteria fit the product category.
* [ ] Ownership costs are considered where relevant.

## Trust

* [ ] Publication date is accurate.
* [ ] Last reviewed date is accurate.
* [ ] No historical claims are fabricated.
* [ ] No unsupported personal claims exist.
* [ ] Affiliate relationship is disclosed.
* [ ] Important limitations are acknowledged.

## Product information

* [ ] Specifications are accurate.
* [ ] Prices are treated as changeable.
* [ ] Availability is treated as changeable.
* [ ] Replacement parts/accessories are considered where relevant.
* [ ] Warranty is considered where relevant.
* [ ] Links lead to the intended product.

## SEO

* [ ] Title tag is clear.
* [ ] H1 is clear.
* [ ] URL is concise.
* [ ] Primary search intent is obvious.
* [ ] Headings are logical.
* [ ] Internal links are useful.
* [ ] Images have appropriate descriptive alt text.
* [ ] Keywords are natural rather than forced.

## Visual

* [ ] Hero image fits the brand.
* [ ] Product images are accurate.
* [ ] Images do not imply fake testing.
* [ ] No generic stock-photo feeling dominates.
* [ ] Visuals support the editorial argument.

## Conversion

* [ ] Affiliate links are easy to find.
* [ ] CTAs are clear but not aggressive.
* [ ] Product recommendations are contextual.
* [ ] The reader knows why each product is recommended.
* [ ] The article does not feel like an Amazon catalog.

---

# 46. The Gold Standard

The ideal Selected by Men article should leave the reader thinking:

> **"They could have recommended more products, but they deliberately didn't."**

Then:

> **"They told me what matters."**

Then:

> **"They told me what the catch is."**

And finally:

> **"I know which one makes sense for me."**

That is the experience we are trying to create.

---

# 47. The Editorial North Star

Selected by Men does not need to be the site with the most products.

It does not need to publish the most articles.

It does not need to pretend to have tested everything.

It does not need to sound like a massive editorial organization.

It needs to become known for one thing:

> **Good judgment, made visible.**

The reader should be able to see the filter.

The sources should be clear.

The limitations should be acknowledged.

The opinions should be defensible.

The commercial relationship should be transparent.

And the recommendations should be selective enough to mean something.

**Buy fewer things. Own better ones.**

That is not only the tagline.

It is the editorial standard.
